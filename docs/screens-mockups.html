1

What I found

I took screenshots of Home, the Queue, Settings, a Roleplay, a Chain, your new Storyboard and the Scenario page at phone (390 px), desktop (1440 px) and wide (1920, 2560 and 3440 px) widths, in the light theme. Here are the problems, in plain terms, most important first.

  1. 1
    The story or the picture gets the leftover space. Every Session screen has a row of settings above its main view (Text Model, Image Model, steps; in a Roleplay also Pictures, Render replies, Score as you play and the music slider) and a row of equal-weight buttons below it. The settings rarely change, but they're in view on every turn.
  2. 2
    The Frames list is a sidebar in the middle, on all three kinds of Session. It does the job a left panel does in every chat app, but it sits between the main view and the Frame panel, so the screen is three columns of similar weight and nothing reads as the main thing.
  3. 3
    The Frame panel is one long column: timings, the Image Prompt, picture buttons, 3D buttons and the clip controls, all the same size. Buttons that act on the picture sit away from the picture.
  4. 4
    The Scenario page is one long form with Start at the very bottom. About twenty Suggest buttons repeat down the page, the choice of Roleplay, Chain or Storyboard comes last although it changes what matters above it, and the description is cut off in one line.
  5. 5
    Settings and the Queue are two different kinds of page: different widths, titles and spacing.
  6. 6
    At phone width the Session screens squash instead of stack, and the header runs over itself. At 3440 px a Roleplay's words and pictures drift to opposite edges. Home's two-column layout waits until 2240 px.
  7. 7
    The "old-school game" feeling comes from boxes, not the colour: blocks with borders inside blocks with borders. The parchment is worth keeping. Dark mode is plain grey, so it has none of the app's character.

What I didn't check: dark-mode screenshots (I read the colours from the code) and a real phone. I opened the Scenario page in my own headless browser, so its draft stays there and doesn't replace yours. The mockups use the public tavern Scenario's names, invented lines and gradients for pictures, not anything from your Sessions.

Sessions: the film strip

The same screen for a Roleplay, a Chain and a Storyboard: the Session's bar, a strip of Frames under it, the picked Frame large, a floating box at the bottom, and a drawer on the right that stays closed until you ask for it. There's no left panel: the strip is the Frames list, turned on its side.

Two things make it work:

  • A Frame with no picture shows its words in its tile (the Message, or the Beat), so the strip is never a row of blanks, and a Roleplay played without Pictures still works.
  • The ▦ button at the end of the strip opens every Frame as a contact sheet with a search box, for finding a moment in a long Session. Its second mode compares Image Models (section 5), which is today's Pictures page.

The Queue is in two places: running work shows on its Frame's tile as a spinner, and the rail's Queue button slides in the rest, with this Session's jobs first.

Settings and the Queue: pages or overlays?

You liked the new shape (sections down the left, rows on the right). As for page or dialog, I'd treat the two differently, because you use them differently:

  • Settings: a large panel over the screen you were on, like Discord's or ChatGPT's. It keeps its own address (/settings/images), so a link or a refresh still opens it, and Esc or the ✕ takes you back exactly where you were. You go to Settings to change one thing and come back, and an overlay says so. A small dialog would be too cramped for Machines or Images.
  • Queue: a panel sliding in from the side when you click the rail's Queue button, over the Session, so you can watch a render while seeing the story. A link in it opens the full Queue page for the long view (every machine, finished jobs and their timings).

Today Settings and the Queue are pages, but the Session behind them is kept alive, so going there and back already loses nothing. The overlay mostly changes how it feels: you're still in the Session.

The look, and a dark mode worth using

Most of the "game UI" feel comes from boxes inside boxes, which the layouts above take out. The colours are a separate choice, and the one thing still open:

My pick is now the soft split. It keeps the parchment everywhere, so the app still looks like nothing else, and it still tells the story apart from the tools, which is what you liked about the split.

Why today's dark mode doesn't work, and a fix

Light mode is parchment with brown ink, a world of its own. Dark mode swaps all of it for Tailwind's plain neutral greys, so it feels like a different, generic app. Suggestions, in order of how much they'd help:

  1. Make it lamplight: the same parchment world at night. Near-black with a brown tint for the background, a lighter brown for panels, and the parchment colour itself as the text. Nothing pure black or pure grey.
  2. Keep the brown ink as the accent. In light mode, buttons and the player's Messages are dark ink on paper. In lamplight they become paper on dark: the same pairing, turned over.
  3. Lower the contrast a touch: off-white text rather than white, which is easier on the eyes at night and makes the pictures, not the words, the brightest thing on screen.
  4. Fewer lines. Borders look heavier in the dark, so panels are told apart by a slightly different background rather than outlines.

Since you prefer light, I'd make the dark mode a quick step at the end, and set the app to start in Light rather than System.

A split that isn't jarring

The first split looked jarring for two reasons, and both can be fixed:

  • It changed colour family. Warm parchment sat against cool grey, so the screen looked like two apps glued together. The soft split stays in one family: the tools are the same paper, only paler and less saturated, and the story is the full parchment. In the dark, the tools are a brown-tinted charcoal and the story is lamplit brown.
  • It had a hard edge. The story was a block of colour running to the screen's edges. Now it's a sheet with rounded corners and a soft shadow, lying on the desk, so the change of tone reads as "a page" rather than as a seam.

So the difference is one of tone, not of colour: enough to see what's the story and what's the tools, with nothing shouting. Try each on the Roleplay screen, and click Prompt on the picture to see the drawer in each:
